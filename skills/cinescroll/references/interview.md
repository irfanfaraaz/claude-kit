# The Interview

Six questions. They exist to extract a **journey and a feeling**, not a feature list. Anyone can ask "what's your brand color." The reason two Cinescroll pages don't look alike is that these answers don't look alike.

Ask all six in one message. Keep your own commentary out of it — a wall of your reasoning before the questions makes people answer defensively.

If recon (phase 1) already answered one, skip it and say you skipped it.

---

## Q1 — The order of belief

> What does the visitor hit first, and what comes after that? Not the sections — the order you want them to believe things in.

Offer 3 concrete openings drawn from *their* actual material, e.g.:
- Lead with proof (numbers, logos, receipts)
- Lead with the founder's story
- Lead with the product doing the thing
- Lead with the problem, stated harder than they'd state it themselves

**Reading the answer:** this sets your section order *and* tells you where the signature moment goes. Whatever they lead with is what deserves the expensive mechanism. If they lead with proof, your signature moment is a data mechanism. If they lead with the product, it's a frame scrub or a pinned reveal.

## Q2 — The one sentence

> What must the visitor believe by the time they reach the bottom? One sentence. Not a feature list.

**Reading the answer:** this is your ruthlessness test for the section map. Every section either moves the visitor toward this sentence or gets cut. If they give you three sentences, pick the one that's hardest to prove and build toward that — the easy claims don't need a page.

If they give you a mission statement full of abstractions ("empower people to..."), push once: *what would make someone believe that who currently doesn't?* The answer to that is the real brief.

## Q3 — What's real

> What assets do you actually have? Photos, screenshots, recordings, real numbers, customer quotes. And which parts are you fine with me generating?

**Reading the answer:** this sets the generate-vs-curate ratio, which is the single biggest driver of whether the page reads as credible or as slop.

Push for more than they offer. People forget they have things. Ask specifically about: screenshots of their own product, photos from events, anything with a real person's face, anything with a real number on it, DMs or reviews they've received.

If the honest answer is "nothing" — say so plainly and design *around* the absence. A page with zero real assets should lean typographic and abstract, not fill the void with generated faces. Generated humans on a trust-driven page are a negative asset.

## Q4 — Calm and loud

> Where should this page feel calm, and where should it feel intense?

**Reading the answer:** this is your pacing map. Most pages fail by being uniformly medium. You need at least one genuinely quiet section — a lot of space, small type, no motion — for the loud moment to register as loud.

If they say "intense throughout," overrule them gently and explain why: constant intensity flattens. Give them one loud peak and one clear valley, then let them see it.

## Q5 — The signature move

> Name one thing this page should do that you haven't seen another site do. It doesn't have to be technically possible — I'll tell you.

**Reading the answer:** this is the highest-value question in the interview and people underrate it. Even a half-formed answer gives you an angle.

Answers tend to fall into a few shapes, and each maps to a mechanism:
- *"Every claim has a source"* → a persistent sidebar counter that unlocks as you scroll
- *"It should feel like flying through it"* → frame-scrubbed camera move
- *"You should see the thing being made"* → pinned build-up sequence
- *"It should know who I am"* → branching content by referrer or a first-scroll choice
- *"It should feel like a document, not an ad"* → editorial layout with data mechanisms

If they draw a blank, propose two based on their business and let them pick. Never skip this question — a page with no signature move is a template with good typography.

## Q6 — The one link that matters

> What's the single action you want them to take, and where does it live?

**Reading the answer:** tells you what everything is structurally pointing at. Get the *actual URL* now, not later — pages routinely ship with the primary CTA pointing at a placeholder because nobody asked.

If they name three actions, ask which one they'd keep if they could only have one. Build for that one and demote the others.

---

## After the answers

Restate the brief back in **five lines maximum**:

```
Opening:    ...
Belief:     ...
Real vs generated:  ...
Peak / valley:      ...
Signature:  ...
```

Ask for one round of correction, then go build the section map. Don't ask for approval twice.
